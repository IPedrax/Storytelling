# Writing that doesn't read as machine-written

Run this as a **revision pass over drafted prose**, not as a style to write in. Draft first using
[prose.md](prose.md); then hunt.

## Why the tells exist

A language model picks the most probable next word. Do that a few thousand times and you get
**the statistical average of everything ever written**: fluent, balanced, tidy, and belonging to
nobody. Every tell below is that averageness becoming visible.

Which means the fix is never a synonym swap. The fix is **commitment**: a specific noun, an
opinion, an odd rhythm, a detail only this narrator would notice. Prose reads as human when
somebody clearly chose it.

Two honest boundaries. First, detectors are unreliable in both directions and are not the target;
writing to beat a classifier produces worse prose than writing to sound like a person. Second, a
tell is not automatically an error. `Not X, but Y` is a real device. The signal is **frequency**:
once in a chapter is voice, three times on a page is a machine.

## Sentence-shape tells

The strongest signals, because readers feel them without being able to name them.

| Pattern | Looks like | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Negated antithesis** | *"It wasn't fear. It was something older."* / *"Not a mirror, but a portal."* | Cut the negation. Say the second half only. The first half was throat-clearing. |
| **Not just X, but Y** | *"Not just a soldier, but a symbol."* | Same. Pick one and commit. |
| **X rather than Y** | *"Choosing silence rather than argument."* | Usually just "She said nothing." |
| **Rule of three** | *"a wound, a warning, a promise"* | Two items, or four. Three is the machine's default cadence. |
| **Trailing participle** | *"...she turned away, marking the end of something."* | Delete the clause. It tells the reader how to feel and adds no information. |
| **Copula avoidance** | *"serves as," "stands as," "functions as"* | "is." |
| **Perfect symmetry** | Two clauses of matched length balanced on a comma, repeatedly. | Break one. Let a sentence be lopsided. |
| **Ornate, then curt** | A long metaphorical sentence, then a two-word fragment. Repeated as a rhythm. | Fragments work once. As a tic they announce themselves. |
| **Summary sentence** | A paragraph's last line restating the paragraph. | Delete it. It is almost always deletable, which is the tell. |

## Vocabulary

Words carrying an unearned association with machine text. Not forbidden, but each one should
survive a deliberate look.

**Near-certain**: delve, tapestry, testament, realm, landscape (figurative), navigate
(figurative), underscore, showcase, foster, bolster, harness, illuminate, intricate, pivotal,
crucial, profound, vibrant, myriad, meticulous, seamless, nestled, boasts, embark, beacon,
crucible, resonate, evoke, palpable, cacophony, symphony (figurative), whispered promise, dance
(figurative: "a dance of shadows").

**Connective tissue**: moreover, furthermore, additionally, indeed, crucially, notably, it is
worth noting, in a world where, little did they know.

**Intensifier fog**: something, somehow, almost, seemed to, a kind of, as if. These hedge the
image instead of making it. *"a kind of quiet fell"* is weaker than *"the room went quiet."*

Note that this list rots. Models shift; "delve" peaked in 2024 and faded. Judge by **frequency
inside your own manuscript**, not by the list alone: any word appearing three times in a chapter
is a tic regardless of what wrote it.

## Fiction-specific tells

Where AI prose gives itself away in a novel rather than in an essay.

**The body-language repertoire.** Machine prose owns about nine gestures and cycles them:
breath hitching, jaw tightening, throat bobbing, eyes narrowing, a hand raking through hair,
knuckles whitening, a smirk, a shiver down the spine, a breath released that was not known to be
held. Real people have idiosyncratic bodies. Give a character **one gesture that is theirs** and
let everyone else be still. Stillness is underused and reads as real.

**Emotion named after it is shown.** *"She slammed the cup down, furious."* Pick one. Naming the
emotion after demonstrating it is the single most common machine habit in fiction, because the
model is hedging that the demonstration failed.

**The compound emotion.** *"a mix of relief and dread," "somewhere between grief and anger."*
Real feeling is specific and usually surprising. Name the odd one instead: she was hungry, which
appalled her.

**Sensory over-service.** Every scene supplying sight, sound, and smell in order. People notice
unevenly and selectively. A frightened character does not catalogue the wallpaper.

**Eyeball kicks.** Metaphors that are elaborate and mean nothing on inspection: *"a geometry of
madness," "silence with teeth."* Test every figure of speech literally. If it does not resolve
into an image, cut it.

**Everyone is articulate.** Machine dialogue is fluent, complete, and responsive. Real speech is
interrupted, evasive, badly timed, and frequently answers a question nobody asked. Let someone
be inarticulate about the thing that matters most.

**Nothing is unresolved.** Every scene closes its own loop; every chapter lands a beat. Human
novels leave things hanging, waste a scene on something that turns out not to matter, and let a
conversation end without a button.

**Names.** `Elara`, `Kael`, `Lyra`, `Seraphina`, `Thorne`, `Anya`, `Cassian` in fantasy;
`Chen` and `Martinez` as the default surnames for "diverse" casts; apostrophes as exotica
(`Ka'lar'eth`). Build names from the world's own naming logic instead (see
[world.md](world.md)). If a name could belong to any book, it belongs to none.

> Worth knowing: the examples inside this skill use `C-Elara`, which is exactly the name on that
> list. That is deliberate as a placeholder and a bad choice as a character. Do not inherit it.

## Structural tells

Above the sentence, where editors notice first.

- **Uniform paragraphs.** Four to six lines, over and over. Real chapters have a one-line paragraph and a page-long one.
- **Even chapters.** Every chapter the same length, every one ending on a hook.
- **The moral.** A closing paragraph that explains what the reader was meant to take from it. Fiction that trusts itself stops one beat earlier.
- **Nobody is boring.** Every character quippy, capable, and interesting. Cast a dull one.
- **The clean spine.** Setup, escalation, and resolution too evenly distributed. Real books are lopsided; something takes over for a hundred pages.
- **No dead air.** People eat, wait, repeat themselves, and discuss the wrong thing. Some of that should survive to the page.

## What human prose has that machine prose lacks

Adding these matters more than deleting the tells.

- **Burstiness.** Sentence length varying hard: four words, then thirty-one, then six. Machine prose regresses to a comfortable middle.
- **Specificity that serves nothing.** The ferryman apologizes to the horses. It goes nowhere. It is why he is real.
- **Opinion in the narration.** Even close third has attitude. A narrator who judges nothing is a camera.
- **The wrong word on purpose.** Diction slightly off in a way that belongs to this narrator and no other.
- **Local knowledge.** Regionalisms, trade jargon, brand names, the way people in one town actually say a thing.
- **Repetition as a choice.** Machines vary synonyms to avoid repeating (elegant variation). Humans repeat a word deliberately, and the repetition lands.
- **Asymmetry of attention.** Three pages on a meal, one line on a battle, because that is what the narrator cared about.
- **Something the author will not explain.** At least one thing on the page that is never accounted for.

## Punctuation

- **Em dashes.** The most-cited tell, and overused by every model. More than one or two in a page is a signal. Rewrite with a colon, a semicolon, parentheses, or two sentences. Some vaults ban them outright; check `CLAUDE.md` and honor it.
- **Semicolons everywhere.** Newer models substituted the semicolon for the em dash and now overuse that instead. Same rule.
- **Bold and italics for emphasis** inside prose. Rare in published fiction. Let the sentence carry the stress.
- **Ellipses as a mood.** Trailing dots to imply depth. Usually a sentence that stopped early.

## The pass

Run in this order on any drafted scene. Stop at the first thing you find and fix it, then continue.

1. Search the text for `not just`, `rather than`, ` but ` after a negation. Cut or rewrite each.
2. Count trailing participle clauses. Target zero.
3. Read only the sentence lengths. If they cluster, break the cluster.
4. Circle every gesture. More than two from the standard repertoire: replace with stillness or something specific to the character.
5. Find each named emotion. Delete it if it was already shown.
6. Test every metaphor literally. Cut what does not resolve.
7. Read the dialogue aloud. Anyone too fluent, make them fail to say it.
8. Delete every paragraph's last sentence, and put back only the ones the paragraph misses.
9. Count em dashes and semicolons per page.
10. Ask what on this page could only have been written by someone who knows this world. If the answer is nothing, the page is not finished.
