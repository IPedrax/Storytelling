# World, culture, groups, religion, systems

A world is believable not when it is complete, but when it is **consistent, costly, and older
than the story.**

## Build a hollow iceberg

The old advice says build the whole iceberg and show the tip. That's how projects die.

Build a **lattice**, not a block: a strong, intricate structure under the surface with a great
deal of empty space in it. The reader needs to believe the world extends past the page, not to
receive it. Depth is an *illusion produced by specificity*, and specificity is cheap.

Practical form: for any element, know **three levels down on one thread** rather than one level
down on thirty. A named street with a named smell and a named grudge does more than a continental
map.

## Sanderson's laws apply past magic

Written for magic systems; they govern any invented system, including politics, tech, and gods.

- **Zeroth:** err on the side of what's awesome. The rules serve the story, not the reverse.
- **First:** *your ability to resolve conflict with a system is proportional to how well the reader understands it.* Solve a problem with a rule the reader knows → satisfying. Solve it with a rule introduced on the spot → cheating. A system the reader doesn't understand can create problems but must not solve them.
- **Second:** **limitations > powers.** What a system *cannot* do, what it costs, and where it fails are more interesting than what it can do. Write the cost before the capability.
- **Third:** **expand what you have before you add something new.** Most worlds are underexploited, not underbuilt. Before inventing a second order of mages, ask what the first one implies that hasn't been used yet.

The Third Law is the cure for worldbuilder's disease. Whenever the impulse is "and also there's
another kingdom", the better move is "what does the kingdom I already have imply about
inheritance, plumbing, and who cleans up?"

## Culture comes from material conditions

Cultures are not flavor packs. They are **answers to what the land makes hard.** Start from
constraint, and belief follows.

Work the chain: **geography → what's scarce → what's abundant → how food is produced → who
controls the surplus → who is owed deference → what that arrangement needs people to believe.**

Then the anthropologist's three questions, applied to whatever matters most in the world
(grain, water, magic, bandwidth, memory):

- **Production**: who makes it, with whose body, using what?
- **Distribution**: how does it move, who takes a cut, what's the unit of exchange?
- **Consumption**: who gets to use it, who watches others use it, what's forbidden?

Then the pressure questions that make a society feel inhabited:

- **Mobility**: can you leave the station you were born into? By what route: marriage, war, priesthood, money, talent? Who resents that route existing?
- **Kinship**: who counts as family, who inherits, who is obliged to whom?
- **Time**: what's the calendar built around? Harvest, tide, holy day, fiscal quarter? What are people always *late for*?
- **The body**: what's shameful to show, what's polite to touch, what do they do with the dead?
- **Deviance**: what's the worst thing you can be here, and what happens to you? A culture is defined by who it exiles.
- **Manners**: one specific piece of etiquette a stranger would get wrong. This single detail carries more culture than three pages of history.

Two traps: cultures are not monoliths (every one contains its own dissidents, its rural/urban
split, its generation gap), and no culture experiences itself as strange. From inside, all of it
is just how things are done.

## Groups and factions

A faction is not a color and a crest. Define each by five things:

1. **Want**: the concrete thing they're trying to get, stated in one sentence.
2. **Leverage**: what they actually control: gold, gates, grain, votes, violence, information, legitimacy.
3. **Constraint**: what they cannot do without losing what they are. The Church can't win by simply killing everyone; the merchants can't win by burning the road.
4. **Fracture**: the internal disagreement that could split them. Every group has a hardline wing and a pragmatist wing, and they hate each other more than they hate the enemy.
5. **Price of exit**: what it costs a member to leave. This is where individual characters get their stakes.

Good factions are **partly right**. If one is simply correct, the conflict is a chore. Give each
one a legitimate grievance and a method that can't be endorsed.

Then check the map: two factions with the same Want and the same Leverage are one faction wearing
two hats; merge them.

## Families

A family is not a group with a nicer name. A guild is joined and can be left; a family is
**inherited and cannot be resigned from**, and that single difference is the entire dramatic
engine. Members who despise each other stay entangled. Obligation outlives affection.

Define one by:

1. **What it holds**: land, a trade, an office, a debt, a secret, a bloodline trait. A family with nothing to hold has nothing to fight over and nothing to lose.
2. **Succession**: who inherits, by what rule, and who is cheated by that rule. Primogeniture, election, merit, the last surviving, the one the dying named. **The rule matters less than who it excludes**; that person is a plot.
3. **The story it tells about itself**: every family has an official version of its own past. It's flattering and partly false, and someone alive knows which part.
4. **The shame**: one thing everyone knows and nobody says. Families are organized around their silences more than their traditions.
5. **The exile**: who left, or was cut off, and whether the door is still open. This is where outside characters get a way in.
6. **Marriage and adoption**: the ways in are as revealing as the ways out. Who is allowed to marry in? What does the family gain by it? Who counts as kin without blood?

Kinship is structural, so keep it as links, not prose: a `family` key on each member, `head` and
`seat` on the family note, and the entanglements (a disputed will, a marriage that bought peace,
a killing nobody was tried for) as `E-` events both sides point at. Do not write a genealogy by
hand in two places; it will disagree with itself within a week.

**Family and group are different edges, and a person carries both.** A Vashti who serves the
Silent Order is interesting exactly because the Order will one day ask for something the family
forbids. Cross the two axes deliberately: the most useful characters sit where two loyalties
can't both be honored.

## Religion

Religions are the most commonly flattened part of a world. Real ones are **ancient, messy,
human**: contradiction, corruption, compassion, zealotry, beauty, and fear all at once.

**Start with function, not mythology.** Before naming a single god, answer what this faith *does*
for believers: explains suffering, licenses power, organizes the calendar, buries the dead,
makes a bad harvest survivable, tells you who to marry. Cosmology is the last thing to invent,
not the first.

Then:

- **Orthodoxy vs orthopraxy**: is a believer defined by what they *believe* or what they *do*? This one choice reshapes everything: who counts as a heretic, what conversion looks like, whether private doubt matters at all.
- **Doctrine vs folk practice**: the official teaching and what villagers actually do. The gap between them is where your most interesting characters live: the priest who tolerates the roadside shrine, the one who doesn't.
- **At least two internal camps** who disagree about something *specific*, not "conservative vs liberal", but "whether the third rite may be performed by women", "whether the prophecy was already fulfilled". Specific disagreement generates plot; vague disagreement generates nothing.
- **Schism history**: every faith that lasts splits. A break centuries back that still shapes borders, and an older rite practiced underground and considered heresy now. Schism produces factions, and factions produce conflict without needing a villain.
- **Embodied practice**: what does a believer do with their *body*? Kneel, fast, cut hair, hold silence, wash hands a specific way, refuse a specific food. Belief that never reaches the body stays abstract on the page. One concrete ritual outperforms a whole cosmology.
- **The cost of faith**: what does it take from the devout? A faith that only comforts is decoration.
- **The nonbelievers**: the indifferent, the merely social attendees, the quietly lapsed. Most people in any faith are not zealots. Their casualness makes the zealots legible.

If gods are demonstrably real in this world, that's an entirely different problem: theology
becomes a natural science, and doubt has to relocate, usually from *whether* they exist to
*whether they deserve worship*.

## Naming and language

- Pick a **sound palette** per culture: which consonants cluster, which vowels dominate, typical word length. Consistency inside a culture, contrast between cultures. That's 90% of the effect.
- Names should mean something *in-world*: patronymics, trades, places, omens, the month of birth. A naming convention implies a society.
- Avoid apostrophe salt. `Ka'lar'eth` reads as generic, not exotic.
- Say it aloud. A name the reader can't pronounce is a name they skip, and a skipped name is a character they never bond with.
- Loanwords are a free history lesson: if one culture's word for "law" comes from another's language, you've just implied a conquest without writing it.

## History

Don't write a timeline. Write **scars**.

For each historical event, only three things matter on the page: what it changed, who still
benefits, and who is still angry. A war nobody's grandmother remembers is trivia. Keep the
timeline as scaffolding if useful, but the *notes* should record living consequences.

Useful shape: the last generation's catastrophe should still be inside living memory for the
oldest characters. That's what makes history operate as pressure rather than backdrop.

## Guardrails against worldbuilder's disease

- **Nothing gets built without a question in the story that needs it.** If no scene needs the tax system, the tax system waits.
- **Expand before adding** (Third Law). Mine what exists.
- Keep an **Open** section in the world note for unanswered questions. Parking a question is progress; inventing filler is not.
- The world is not the story. If a month goes by and only the world grew, that's the disease.
- Detail earns its place by being *touched*: a currency the characters spend, a road they travel, a rite they endure.

## Note shape

Organic: `W-World.md` alone until sections burst.

```markdown
---
language: pt-BR
---

# W-World

One paragraph: what this place is and what's wrong with it right now.

## The pressure
What's scarce, who controls it, and what that forces people to believe.

## Systems
Magic/tech: what it costs and cannot do (write limits before powers).

## Groups
[[G-Name]]: want · leverage · constraint · fracture · price of exit.

## Faith
Function first. Two camps and their specific disagreement.

## Scars
Events that still hurt someone alive.

## Voice of the place
Manners, taboo, one etiquette a stranger gets wrong. Sound palette for names.

## Open
Unanswered questions. Park them here.
```

Characters go in their own `C-` folders inside this one, never in the world note.
