# Plot and structure

Plot is not a sequence of events. Plot is **causality under pressure**: each event forces the
next, and the pressure rises because the character keeps choosing wrong.

## The causality test

Run this first on any outline. Say the beats aloud with connectors:

- **"and then"** between beats → broken. That's a list, not a plot.
- **"therefore"** or **"but"** → working. Consequence or obstruction.

Every "and then" is a place where the story could stop and nobody would notice. Fix it by making
the previous beat *cause* the next, or cut it.

## Turning points

A turning point is where the action turns because of something a character **does or fails to
do**, and the turn goes somewhere the reader wasn't pointed. Not something that happens *to*
them — that's weather.

Seven-point spine with rough placement (percentages are gravity, not law):

| Beat | ~% | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hook | 0 | The character in their Lie, in motion, before the trouble. |
| Inciting incident | 12 | The disturbance. They can still refuse. |
| First plot point | 25 | They commit. The door locks behind them. Usually a *choice*, not an event. |
| Midpoint | 50 | A revelation flips the terms — reaction becomes action, or the goal proves false. |
| Second plot point | 75 | The lowest point delivers the last piece needed. Everything is lost *and* they now know. |
| Climax | 88 | Want vs Need forced into one choice. |
| Resolution | 100 | The new normal, priced. |

Two rules that matter more than the table: the **first plot point is a decision** (things merely
happening to a protagonist reads as passivity), and the **midpoint changes the character's
relationship to the goal**, not just the odds.

## Story circle (character-driven alternative)

Eight steps, a loop rather than a line:

1. A character is in a zone of comfort — 2. but they want something — 3. they enter an unfamiliar
situation — 4. adapt to it — 5. get what they wanted — 6. pay a heavy price — 7. return to the
familiar situation — 8. having changed.

Use this when the story is *about a person* rather than *about an event*. It maps cleanly onto
the seven points (3 = first plot point, 5 = midpoint, 6 = second plot point) — they're the same
skeleton seen from inside and outside.

## Try-fail cycles

Before a victory that matters, the character earns it by failing. **A problem solved on the first
attempt was not a big enough problem.** Three cycles is the usual dose for a novel-scale goal;
two for a subplot.

Fail in the two productive ways, alternating:

- **"Yes, but"** — they get it, and it costs or complicates. *Yes, she reaches the archive — but the guard now knows her face.*
- **"No, and"** — they don't get it, and it's worse. *No, the archive is sealed — and the sealing was ordered in her name.*

Avoid flat "no". A failure that changes nothing is a delay, and readers feel delay as padding.

The failures must **teach**. Each attempt should reveal something true about the obstacle, so the
final success is earned by understanding, not by dice.

## Scene and sequel

The unit of a story isn't the chapter; it's the pair.

**Scene** (action):
1. **Goal** — what they want *in this scene*, tied to the story goal.
2. **Conflict** — what obstructs it.
3. **Disaster** — they don't get it, or get it at a price. The scene turns.

**Sequel** (reaction):
1. **Reaction** — emotional, physical, immediate. Where interiority lives.
2. **Dilemma** — all options are bad. This is the part writers skip, and skipping it is why
   protagonists feel like they're on rails.
3. **Decision** — a new goal, which becomes the next scene's Goal.

Sequels can compress to a paragraph or a line at pace, but if they vanish entirely the reader
stops believing the character is thinking.

**Diagnostic:** a scene where the POV character wants nothing is not a scene. Either give them a
goal or fold the information into a scene that has one.

## Promise, progress, payoff

Every thread is a contract with the reader.

- **Promise** — the early signal of what this story will deliver (tone, question, danger).
- **Progress** — visible movement; the reader must be able to tell they're closer or further, not merely busy.
- **Payoff** — delivery of the thing promised, in a form better than expected but recognizably the same thing.

Most "sagging middle" problems are **progress** problems, not idea problems: the story is still
moving but the reader can't measure it. Give them a scoreboard — a countdown, a shrinking
resource, a list being crossed off, an approaching date.

Setups and payoffs: plant *before* it's needed and let it seem like something else. A payoff for
an unplanted setup is a cheat; a setup with no payoff is a promise broken. When a solution
arrives, the reader should feel *"of course"*, never *"convenient"*.

## Subplot

The strongest subplots are the **theme argued from another position**. Take the protagonist's
Lie and give it to someone else at a different stage — further along, or refusing it. Now the
subplot comments on the main plot without anyone making a speech.

Structurally: subplots should braid — resolve one just before the main climax so its resolution
*changes the terms* of the final choice.

## Ending discipline

- Close what you opened. Whatever question the opening posed is the question the ending answers.
- The climax should be a **choice**, not a capability. Winning by being stronger is an event; winning by choosing what to lose is a story.
- The protagonist must be the one who acts. Rescue at the climax rewrites the whole book as someone else's story.
- Price the ending. What did the victory cost, and who is still paying?

## Stress test — "does this plot hold up?"

Run in order. Stop at the first failure and fix that.

1. **Causality** — any "and then"?
2. **Agency** — how many turns does the protagonist *cause*? If under half, they're a passenger.
3. **Antagonist plan** — write the antagonist's plan assuming the protagonist never existed. Does it succeed? If it collapses on its own, there was no threat. Does it ignore the protagonist entirely? Then the protagonist is optional.
4. **The obvious solution** — why doesn't the character just do the sensible thing? There must be a reason from *character* (the Lie) or *world* (a hard constraint). "They didn't think of it" is not a reason.
5. **Stakes escalation** — does the cost of failure grow, or just the noise? Bigger explosions aren't bigger stakes; bigger *losses* are.
6. **Coincidence audit** — coincidence may cause trouble, never solve it.
7. **Information flow** — track who knows what, when. Most plot holes are actually knowledge-timeline errors.
8. **The idiot test** — would a competent person in this world short-circuit the plot in one move? If yes, the world needs a constraint or the plan needs a flaw.
9. **Ending contract** — does the climax answer the opening's question?
10. **Cut test** — remove any scene. If nothing downstream breaks, it wasn't load-bearing.

## Failure modes

- **Episodic drift** — well-written scenes that don't compound. The causality test catches it.
- **Reactive protagonist** — they only respond. Give them a plan of their own, even a bad one.
- **Withheld information as tension** — mystery built purely on the narrator hiding what the POV character knows. Cheat. Build tension from *conflicting wants*, not from concealment.
- **The competent middle** — everything goes roughly right for 100 pages. Insert failure.
- **Climax by revelation** — the truth comes out and the plot just stops. Revelations should force *choices*, not substitute for them.
- **Villain monologue as structure** — if the antagonist has to explain the plot, the plot wasn't dramatized.
